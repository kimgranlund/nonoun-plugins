# Bidirectional design reasoning

Design a credible relationship between desired outcomes and the means available to achieve them, within the relevant context and constraints.

## Two complementary directions

**Outside-in:** Start with the surrounding situation and the progress that matters. Infer what the experience needs to make understandable, possible or dependable for that progress to occur.

**Inside-out:** Start with the available content, capabilities, information, interactions and resources. Infer what outcomes they can credibly support, what they require from people or other systems, and where their limits lie.

Use both directions to question and improve the proposed relationship. They are lenses, not assigned stakeholder roles or mandatory sequential phases. User, organizational and operational interests may inform either direction. There may be several participants, competing outcomes, or no meaningful conflict at all.

“Outside” and “inside” are relative to the design boundary. Apply the lenses to an entire experience, a journey, a surface, a state or a component when that level helps the decision. The subject may be understanding, discovery, creation, coordination, evaluation, action or recovery. Do not force every intent into a transaction, workflow or conversion.

## Reconcile intent and means

Look for consequential relationships and mismatches: an outcome without adequate support, a capability without a useful purpose, a promise without evidence, a hidden dependency, avoidable effort, or conflicting incentives. Available capabilities can reveal valuable possibilities; desired outcomes can reveal missing capabilities. Neither direction should merely rationalize an existing layout or feature inventory.

A useful response might change the structure, explanation, interaction, capability, scope or objective. It might remove something, preserve a legitimate non-action, or leave a tension explicit pending evidence. Reconciliation does not require compromise or a claim that every interest has been satisfied. Distinguish binding constraints from inherited conventions before treating either as fixed.

## Reason freely; communicate the decision

Choose the depth, sequence and representation that fit the uncertainty. Move between the lenses as needed. Use stories, maps, briefs, hypotheses or sketches where helpful; no particular terminology, matrix, number of passes or separate agents is required.

Communicate the useful conclusions: the intended outcome, the means proposed to support it, the consequential tradeoff or assumption, and what evidence would help assess the fit. Keep rationale proportionate to the decision. There is no requirement to expose a step-by-step reasoning transcript or mechanically justify every decorative detail.

When producing alternatives, vary a consequential hypothesis about the relationship between intent and means. Keep relevant facts and constraints comparable. Alternatives may explore different explanations, structures, interactions or divisions of responsibility; cosmetic variation alone does not establish a different hypothesis.

Evaluate in both directions: is the intended outcome adequately supported, and does the proposed support have a defensible purpose? Identify where the design could fail or shift costs elsewhere. Treat KPIs as imperfect evidence about outcomes, alongside qualitative observation and guardrails, rather than substitutes for intent. Mark unverified effects and prototype limits explicitly.

Stop elaborating when the available explanation and evidence are sufficient for the next decision. Revisit the relationship when new evidence or constraints change it.
